### Abstract

<s>In the time it takes to read this abstract, your body will have synthesised over five quintillion proteins. Shaped by water, polypeptide chains intricately fold to orchestrate the complex symphony of life. Yet despite decades of structural biology, one fundamental aspect of these molecular machines remains chronically overlooked: their surprising fragility. Combining hydrolytic rate estimates from laboratory heating experiments with empirical data from battered and bruised archaeological proteins reveals that within seven hours of synthesis, one in every five hundred newly minted proteins has already snapped in two. Our fundamental understanding of non-enzymatic peptide bond stability has advanced remarkably little since Walter Schroeder highlighted the problem to Linus Pauling in 1951.</s> For over two decades, the diagenetic survival of ancient bone collagen has been modelled using an Arrhenius activation energy of *E*<sub>a</sub> ≈ 173 kJ·mol⁻¹, derived from high-temperature laboratory heating experiments (55–140 °C). While this parameter describes the acute hydrothermal denaturation (gelatinisation) of powdered fibrils in excess water, its extrapolation across geological deep time produces severe physical anomalies: it predicts multi-million-year survival in sub-zero polar permafrost (>50–70 Ma at -10.5 °C), whereas empirical bone collagen recoveries on Earth terminate at approximately 3.4–3.9 Ma (the High Arctic camel and Ellesmere Island giant bear). Furthermore, it fails to reconcile Early Pleistocene dentin collagen survival at Dmanisi (1.77 Ma, +11.0 °C) with global archaeological radiocarbon boundaries. Here, by coupling <s>a harmonised dataset of 185,189 radiocarbon determinations (18,101 purified bone collagen dates paired with 18,101 non-collagenous organic controls)</s> **an expanded, audited dataset of over 313,000 unique global radiocarbon determinations—from which we draw a primary analytical cohort of 59,678 bone collagen dates (20,500 explicit ultrafiltered determinations and 39,178 probable collagen determinations from uncremated bone) and 163,321 non-collagenous organic controls**—to continuous 120,000-year HadCM3 paleoclimate reanalyses, we deconvolve the primary cause of this discrepancy. We demonstrate that instrumental radiocarbon background blank censoring at ~ 42–45 ka BP imposes an artificial horizontal ceiling on apparent collagen survival for all sites with paleotemperature T̄ < 13.8 °C. Inverting <s>only</s> the uncensored diagenetic regime (T̄ >= 13.8 °C), where maximum collagen survival collapses <s>exponentially to <3 ka</s> **continuously across 15 warm bins to <8.8 ka** at +25 °C <s>reveals the true empirical activation energy of spontaneous bone collagen backbone hydrolysis in burial environments to be *E*<sub>a</sub> = 133.4 ± 3.6 kJ·mol⁻¹ (upper-bound envelope 137.0 kJ·mol⁻¹)</s> **while charcoal controls remain dated to >41 ka, establishes an empirical upper-bound barrier for open-system bone collagen hydrolysis of *E*<sub>a</sub> = 82.2 ± 16.6 kJ·mol⁻¹ (Bin Maximum) and 60.4 ± 8.0 kJ·mol⁻¹ (*Q*<sub>95</sub>)**. This demonstrates that the effective barrier governing spontaneous backbone hydrolysis in burial environments is substantially lower than the 173 kJ·mol⁻¹ denaturation model, converging toward the activation energy of uncatalysed peptide bond hydrolysis in condensed aqueous phases (~80–120 kJ·mol⁻¹). Ortner's intact-bone heating series (*E*<sub>a</sub> = 132.1 kJ·mol⁻¹) remains the most solid physical laboratory anchor for whole skeletal elements, whereas pulverisation and cryogenic freezer milling artificially inflate apparent activation energies to 173–183 kJ·mol⁻¹ by triggering cooperative uncoiling ("polymer-in-a-box" transition). Remarkably, this whole-bone collagen barrier converges with the empirical activation energy of ancient DNA degradation in bone (*E*<sub>a</sub> ≈ 127 kJ·mol⁻¹), pointing to shared matrix-mediated constraints. In contrast, closed intra-crystalline niches (enamel, dentino-enamel junction, petrous bone, and calcite-cemented karst breccias) physically "straightjacket" collagen, preventing uncoiling and fragment loss. Finally, extrapolating these empirical kinetics to physiological temperature (37 °C) demonstrates that in non-renewing human tissues (articular cartilage, dentin, tendons), spontaneous non-enzymatic peptide bond hydrolysis continuously cleaves millions of bonds daily, establishing an inescapable biophysical driver for cumulative lifetime tissue fatigue and osteoarthritis.

## 1. Introduction: The <s>Fragility of Proteins & The</s> Deep-Time <s>Paradox</s> Kinetics of Ancient Proteins

<s>In the time it takes you to read this sentence, your body will have synthesised over five quintillion proteins. Proteins are the fundamental building blocks of life that orchestrate the complex symphony of biological processes. The chains of amino acid residues are shaped by water, intricately folding to minimise the disruption of hydrophobic residues and maximise hydrophilic interactions within this dynamic, hydrogen-bonded network. It is truly remarkable that despite all the attention paid to proteins across modern structural biology and biochemistry, one aspect of these marvels of molecular engineering is constantly overlooked: **their surprising fragility**.</s>

<s>Combining estimates of non-enzymatic hydrolysis (i.e., disintegration by water) from laboratory heating experiments with empirical evidence from battered and bruised ancient proteins from archaeological sites leads to a startling conclusion: **within seven hours, one in every five hundred of your newly minted proteins will have snapped in two**. As the gravedigger memorably observes in Shakespeare's *Hamlet* (Act 5, Scene 1):</s>

<s>> *"A tanner will last you nine year... his hide is so tann'd with his trade, that he will keep out water a great while; and your water is a sore decayer of your whoreson dead body."*</s>

<s>What, which, how, when, where? The truth is—we simply do not know.</s> Proteins are the structural and functional workhorses of life, assembled from linear polypeptide chains that fold into intricate three-dimensional architectures shaped by solvent water. Despite the central importance of proteins across structural biology, molecular evolution, and medicine, fundamental understanding of their long-term chemical durability under non-enzymatic conditions has remained remarkably unresolved. Seven decades after Walter Schroeder first highlighted the puzzle of spontaneous peptide bond cleavage in a memorandum to Linus Pauling [1], predicting the non-enzymatic survival of polypeptide backbones across physiological to geological timescales continues to pose formidable theoretical and analytical challenges [2, 3].

In the early 1990s, research into ancient protein survival was primarily driven by amino acid racemisation (AAR) geochronology and the ambition to date Quaternary fossil remains [4, 5]. It was quickly recognised that the kinetics of peptide bond hydrolysis represented the primary rate-limiting bottleneck governing both amino acid release and the loss of structural integrity in biominerals [4, 5]. However, the analytical limitations of Edman degradation and early chromatography made comprehensive kinetic characterisation in complex geological matrices inaccessible. This led our group to help pioneer the field of **‘palaeoproteomics’** in 2014 [6], utilising high-resolution soft-ionisation tandem mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF-MS and LC-MS/MS) to extract, sequence, and identify ancient protein sequences from fossil bones, teeth, eggshells, and cultural artefacts spanning the Pleistocene and Neogene [6, 7].

While mass spectrometry hardware and data acquisition methodologies (DDA, DIA) have advanced exponentially, ancient skeletal proteins persist as severely damaged, truncated, deamidated, and crosslinked polypeptide fragments [8, 9]. Standard database search engines match only a small fraction (<5%) of typical palaeoproteomic tandem mass spectra against modern reference proteomes, leaving a vast reservoir of "dark matter" spectra generated by accumulated diagenetic modifications [9, 10]. Understanding the intrinsic chemical kinetics that generate this degradation is essential for establishing rigorous preservation baselines.

### 1.1 The Classical Thermal Age Framework
Because chemical degradation reactions are fundamentally kinetic processes, chronological age alone is an unreliable predictor of biomolecular survival. Instead, preservation is governed by the integrated thermal history of the burial matrix. This principle was introduced by Colin I. Smith and colleagues in 2001 (*Nature*, "Neanderthal DNA: Not old but old and cold?") [11] and formalised in 2003 (*Journal of Human Evolution*) [12] through the development of the **Thermal Age** framework. 

Thermal age standardises the fluctuating, non-linear paleotemperature trajectory experienced by a fossil specimen into an equivalent duration at a constant reference temperature (conventionally 10 °C, expressed as ka @ 10 °C or Ma @ 10 °C):

$$\mathrm{Age}_{\mathrm{thermal}} = \int_0^{t_{\mathrm{cal}}} \exp\left[ -\frac{E_a}{R}\left( \frac{1}{T(t)} - \frac{1}{T_{\mathrm{ref}}} \right) \right] dt \tag{1}$$

where *E*<sub>a</sub> is the activation energy of the primary rate-limiting degradation pathway, *R* is the universal gas constant (8.31446 J·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹), *T*(*t*) is the absolute temperature (Kelvin) experienced by the fossil at time *t*, and *T*<sub>ref</sub> = 283.15 K (10.0 °C). Because Arrhenius reaction rates scale exponentially with temperature:

$$k(T) = A \exp\left(-\frac{E_a}{RT}\right) \tag{2}$$

brief, warm summer periods inflict exponentially more cumulative hydrolytic damage than long, freezing winters can prevent [12, 13]. Consequently, simple mean annual temperature (MAT) substantially underestimates diagenetic decay in seasonal or periglacial environments.

### 1.2 The *E*<sub>a</sub> = 173 kJ·mol⁻¹ Legacy & Its Deep-Time Breakdown
For more than two decades, the standard parameterisation of bone collagen diagenesis within thermal age modelling has assumed an activation energy of ***E*<sub>a</sub> ≈ 173 kJ·mol⁻¹** (with pre-exponential factor *A* ≈ 2.11 × 10¹⁹ s⁻¹) [13, 14]. This benchmark was derived from elevated-temperature laboratory heating experiments conducted on powdered bone collagen across 55–95 °C and 100–140 °C [13, 15, 16]. At these elevated hydrothermal temperatures, the physical process governing mass loss is the **structural unfolding (denaturation/gelatinisation)** of the triple helix and the rapid solubilisation of gelatin fragments [13, 17].

However, when *E*<sub>a</sub> = 173 kJ·mol⁻¹ is extrapolated across geological timescales into ambient and sub-zero paleotemperature regimes, it generates two severe, irreconcilable physical paradoxes:

1. **The Arctic Deep-Time Paradox:** In sub-zero permafrost environments—such as the Pliocene High Arctic deposits of Ellesmere Island (MAT ≈ -10.5 °C)—an activation energy of 173 kJ·mol⁻¹ predicts an exponential suppression of degradation rate constants to ln(*k* [s⁻¹]) ≈ -39.5 to -45.0. This implies that bone collagen should routinely survive intact for **>50 to 70 Million years** in polar sediments. In empirical reality, the oldest authentic Type I bone collagen ever recovered anywhere on Earth—the High Arctic camel from the Fyles Leaf Bed (~ 3.4 Ma) [18] and the associated Ellesmere Island giant bear (*NUFV 303*, ~ 3.9 Ma) [18]—exhibits an effective degradation rate constant of ln(*k* [s⁻¹]) ≈ -32.0. Beyond ~ 4 Ma, bone collagen is entirely absent from the fossil record, even under continuous permafrost preservation [18, 19]. The 173 kJ·mol⁻¹ laboratory model overestimates the deep-time survival horizon of bone collagen in cold regions by more than an order of magnitude.
2. **The Temperate-to-Subtropical Discrepancy:** In temperate-to-warm environments, *E*<sub>a</sub> = 173 kJ·mol⁻¹ creates severe internal contradictions between archaeological and palaeontological benchmarks. If 173 kJ·mol⁻¹ is anchored to the empirical radiocarbon boundary in temperate Europe (41.2 ka at 14.0 °C), it predicts that bone collagen at Dmanisi, Georgia (*T* ≈ 11.0 °C) should completely disappear within 89–139 ka—yet well-preserved dentin Type I collagen is recovered at Dmanisi at **1.77 Ma** [20]. If the curve is instead anchored to Dmanisi, moving to the subtropical cave temperatures of Swartkrans (15.1–16.5 °C) accelerates decay by 3.5×, predicting a maximum survival limit of only 440–625 ka [21, 22].

### 1.3 Resolving the Discrepancy
Here, as part of the **ChronoProtein** initiative to construct a universal model of protein degradation kinetics, we resolve these longstanding paradoxes. By compiling, harmonising, and auditing over **313,000 global radiocarbon determinations** coupled with continuous 120,000-year HadCM3 paleoclimate trajectories, we demonstrate that the apparent failure of field diagenetic models is an artifact of **instrumental radiocarbon blank truncation**. Deconvolving this instrumental ceiling across an expanded cohort of 59,678 collagen determinations demonstrates that open-system bone collagen diagenesis operates under an activation barrier far lower than the powdered-bone 173 kJ·mol⁻¹ laboratory model. By evaluating this empirical barrier alongside intact-bone laboratory benchmarks, ancient DNA degradation kinetics, biophysical uncoiling models, and dense mineral straightjacketing, we unify radiocarbon chronologies, Middle Pleistocene hominins, and Pliocene Arctic horizons into a single, rigorous biophysical framework.

## 2. Theory & Biophysical Mechanisms of Collagen Degradation

### 2.1 The Multi-Mechanism Nature of Collagen Diagenesis
Bone collagen loss in the burial environment proceeds through distinct, temperature-dependent physical and chemical regimes (Table 1):

1. **Hydrothermal Denaturation / Gelatinisation (*T* > 55 °C) & The "Polymer-in-a-Box" Model:**
   In acute laboratory heating experiments conducted on powdered collagen in excess water, heat disrupts the hydrogen-bonded water networks stabilising the polyproline II triple helix, leading to cooperative unwinding into random-coil gelatin [13, 17]. As established by **Chris A. Miles** and colleagues in their foundational **"Polymer-in-a-Box"** model [26, 57, 58], collagen molecules inside intact fibrillar lattices are sterically confined by adjacent triple helices. Denaturation is an all-or-none cooperative transition that requires the simultaneous rupture of dozens of hydrogen bonds to overcome the free energy barrier of uncoiling against neighboring molecules [26, 57]. For an individual cooperative melting domain, this yields an activation energy of 1.0–1.5 MJ·mol⁻¹, translating to a macroscopic apparent activation energy of *E*<sub>a</sub> ≈ 155–183 kJ·mol⁻¹ with an enormous pre-exponential factor (*A* ≈ 10¹⁹–10²⁰ s⁻¹) [13, 15, 26]. Crucially, this cooperative melting regime only occurs when thermal energy exceeds the denaturation transition temperature (*T*<sub>m</sub> > 40–65 °C).

2. **Spontaneous Backbone Peptide Bond Hydrolysis (-15 °C < T < 35 °C):**
   At ambient burial temperatures, collagen fibrils remain well below their denaturation transition temperature (*T*<sub>m</sub> ≈ 40–42 °C for soluble monomer, *T*<sub>m</sub> > 65 °C for mineralised fibril) [27, 28, 59]. Thermal cooperative melting is physically impossible. Degradation is rate-limited by slow, spontaneous, uncatalysed peptide bond hydrolysis along the polypeptide backbone [2, 3, 29]. The activation energy for uncatalysed amide hydrolysis in solution is *E*<sub>a</sub> ≈ 90–115 kJ·mol⁻¹ [2, 3, 29]. In whole bone, intermolecular covalent crosslinks and intrafibrillar hydroxyapatite encapsulation restrict water access, yielding an effective activation barrier of *E*<sub>a</sub> ≈ 80–132 kJ·mol⁻¹ [15, 31, 32].

3. **Localized Fibril Failure & Diketopiperazine (DKP) Formation:**
   Direct transmission electron microscopy (TEM) investigations by **Hannah E. C. Koon** and colleagues [60, 61, 62] into archaeological bone collagen degradation demonstrate that low-temperature decay does not proceed via global melting of fibrils. Instead, tight lateral packing restricts entropic unwinding, confining initial damage to localized microstructural disruptions. When a single peptide bond undergoes hydrolytic cleavage, it generates a free N-terminus. Because of the high frequency of proline and 4-hydroxyproline in the collagen repeating triplet (Gly-Pro-Hyp), when cleavage leaves a Pro or Hyp residue at the *N*+1 or *N*+2 position, the backbone readily undergoes spontaneous intramolecular aminolysis to form cyclic **diketopiperazines (DKPs)** [63, 64]. This spontaneous cyclisation excises cyclic dipeptides from the free terminus, driving irreversible, localized chain disintegration and facilitating progressive peptide fragmentation even within intact fibrillar bundles.

4. **Closed-System "Straightjacketing" (Dental Enamel, DEJ, Petrous Bone & Karst Breccias):**
   In dense, highly mineralised skeletal tissues—notably mature dental enamel, the dentino-enamel junction (DEJ), the otic capsule of the petrous bone [65], and calcite-cemented karst breccias [44]—micro-porosity is exceptionally low (<1% pore volume) and groundwater leaching is physically excluded [20, 33, 65]. The dense mineral crystallites physically **"straightjacket"** the entrapped collagen and non-collagenous proteins, leaving insufficient physical volume for triple-helix uncoiling or peptide fragment swelling. As documented across the extensive closed-system amino acid racemisation (AAR) literature [5, 25, 33, 66], cleaved peptide fragments cannot escape the mineral crystallites; they remain mechanically trapped and hydrolyse in a closed intra-crystalline micro-reactor, enabling sequence identification at thermal ages exceeding 10–25 Ma @ 10 °C [19, 20, 22].

### 2.2 The Influence of Sample Preparation: Chunks vs Milled Powder
The sharp divergence in laboratory-derived activation energies between Ortner et al. (1972) (*E*<sub>a</sub> = 132.1 kJ·mol⁻¹) and subsequent studies by Von Endt & Ortner (1984) (*E*<sub>a</sub> = 183.3 kJ·mol⁻¹) and Smith (2002) (*E*<sub>a</sub> = 173.2 kJ·mol⁻¹) is directly explained by sample preparation:

- **Ortner, Von Endt & Robinson (1972)** heated **intact cortical bone chunks** cut directly from bovine and human long bones. The unbroken mineralised macro-architecture constrained water activity, throttled diffusion, and prevented cooperative unfolding, yielding *E*<sub>a</sub> = 132.1 kJ·mol⁻¹.
- **Von Endt & Ortner (1984)** specifically evaluated the effect of bone pulverisation across the same temperature regime (100–140 °C), demonstrating that grinding bone into fine powder (<63 µm) increased the activation energy by **+51.2 kJ·mol⁻¹** (to 183.3 kJ·mol⁻¹).
- **Colin Smith (2002)** utilized **cryogenically freezer-milled bone powder** (Spex 6700 mill under liquid nitrogen, sieved to <63 µm). As Smith noted (§2.2, p. 1213) [13], cryogenic milling and intense mechanical shear inflict severe microstructural disruption and ice-crystal fracturing, cleaving stabilizing crosslinks and allowing instantaneous bulk swelling and cooperative denaturation upon heating (*E*<sub>a</sub> = 173.2 kJ·mol⁻¹).

Mechanical pulverisation destroys the protective mineral confinement, converting a matrix-throttled hydrolytic process into an unconstrained, high-energy cooperative melting transition.

### 2.3 Mathematical Derivation of the Radiocarbon Truncation Artifact
Let *C*(*t*) be the residual fraction of intact bone collagen remaining in a fossil at calendar age *t*<sub>cal</sub>, with initial concentration *C*₀. Assuming pseudo-first-order degradation kinetics:

$$\ln\left(\frac{C(t_{\mathrm{cal}})}{C_0}\right) = - \int_0^{t_{\mathrm{cal}}} k(T(t))\, dt = - k(T_{\mathrm{ref}}) \cdot \mathrm{Age}_{\mathrm{thermal}} \tag{3}$$

For an archaeological bone to yield a valid collagen radiocarbon date, it must retain at least a critical threshold fraction of collagen, conventionally *C*/*C*₀ >= 0.01 (1% residual yield, equivalent to >= 0.2% collagen by weight of whole bone) [34, 35]. Setting *C*/*C*₀ = 0.01:

$$\mathrm{Age}_{\mathrm{thermal, limit}} = \frac{-\ln(0.01)}{k(T_{\mathrm{ref}})} = \frac{4.60517}{k(10^\circ\mathrm{C})} \tag{4}$$

In a constant or paleoclimate-integrated effective temperature regime T̄, the calendar time *t*<sub>limit</sub> required to reach this 1% yield threshold is:

$$t_{\mathrm{limit}}(\bar{T}) = \frac{4.60517}{A} \exp\left(\frac{E_a}{R(\bar{T} + 273.15)}\right) \tag{5}$$

Taking the natural logarithm:

$$\ln\left(t_{\mathrm{limit}}\right) = \left[\ln\left(\frac{4.60517}{A}\right)\right] + \frac{E_a}{R}\left(\frac{1}{\bar{T} + 273.15}\right) \tag{6}$$

However, Accelerator Mass Spectrometry (AMS) radiocarbon measurements are subject to an independent **instrumental background blank ceiling** (*t*<sub>AMS, blank</sub> ≈ 42,000–45,000 BP), dictated by modern carbon contamination during sample prep and detector physics [36, 37]. Therefore, the observed maximum calendar age *t*<sub>obs,max</sub>(T̄) across archaeological databases is:

$$t_{\mathrm{obs, max}}(\bar{T}) = \min\left( t_{\mathrm{limit}}(\bar{T}),\ t_{\mathrm{AMS, blank}} \right) \tag{7}$$

The crossover temperature *T*<sub>c</sub> occurs where *t*<sub>limit</sub>(*T*<sub>c</sub>) = *t*<sub>AMS, blank</sub> ≈ 42.0 ka:

$$\frac{1}{T_c + 273.15} = \frac{R}{E_a} \ln\left( \frac{t_{\mathrm{AMS, blank}} \cdot A}{4.60517} \right) \tag{8}$$

For bone collagen, *T*<sub>c</sub> ≈ 13.8 °C. Consequently:
- **For cold/temperate sites (T̄ < 13.8 °C):** Bone collagen is abundantly preserved for >42 ka, but all dates are truncated at 42–45 ka by AMS physics.
- **For warm sites (T̄ >= 13.8 °C):** Collagen decays before reaching the 42 ka instrumental boundary (*t*<sub>limit</sub> < 42 ka). This regime is **completely uncensored** and reflects pure Arrhenius hydrolysis kinetics.

## 3. Materials & Computational Methods

### 3.1 Expanded Global Radiocarbon Compilation & Material Reclassification
We compiled a comprehensive global database of **313,248 unique geolocated radiocarbon determinations** (from 639,530 raw records) by integrating 12 primary global and regional repositories:
1. **XRONOS (Roe et al. 2025)** [47]: Open global archaeological chronology infrastructure (incorporating CARD, EUROEVOL, RADON, etc.).
2. **MEGA14C (Herrando-Pérez et al. 2026)** [41]: Curated Holarctic mammal bone collagen with verified C:N chemistry.
3. **p3k14c (Bird et al. 2022/2024)** [40]: Global archaeological radiocarbon dataset.
4. **14C Palaeolithic Europe (Vermeersch 2026)** [48]: Upper and Middle Palaeolithic determinations across Europe.
5. **CalPal (Cologne Radiocarbon Database 2020)** [49]: Global Upper Palaeolithic and Holocene determinations.
6. **Radon-B (Kiel University)** [50]: Central, Northern, and Eastern European Neolithic and Bronze Age series.
7. **RXPAND / BANADAE (Gregorio de Souza et al.)** [51]: South American archaeological radiocarbon series.
8. **AustArch (ADS / Williams et al.)** [52]: Australian and Sahul archaeological chronology.
9. **14SEA (Southeast Asia Database)** [53]: Southeast Asian and Island Southeast Asian series.
10. **SARD (Loftus et al. 2021)** [54]: Southern African Radiocarbon Database.
11. **Caribbean 14C (Riris et al.)** [55]: Caribbean archaeological series.
12. **aDRAC (Seidensticker et al.)** [56]: Central African radiocarbon records.

#### 3.1.1 Deduplication & Quality Control Protocol
Because global compilations aggregate regional databases, identical determinations frequently appear multiple times under minor naming variations. We implemented an automated, multi-tiered deduplication pipeline:
1. Exact matching on normalised laboratory identifier codes (e.g., `OxA-`, `Beta-`, `GrA-`, `UGAMS-`).
2. Spatial-temporal clustering: records sharing identical geographical coordinates (to 4 decimal places), identical uncalibrated ¹⁴C ages, and identical standard errors were consolidated into single master determinations.
3. Quality filtering: all records lacking geographical coordinates, standard errors, or explicit material descriptions were excluded.

#### 3.1.2 Material Classification & Organic Controls
Routine radiocarbon laboratory practice is to extract and date collagen; the `BONE_UNDIFFERENTIATED` class in international compilations represents a metadata-reporting gap rather than an alternative chemistry. The sole exception is **cremated or calcined bone**, which is dated on the structural carbonate of calcined bioapatite and behaves as an entirely different material. 

We established three primary analytical classes:
- **`COLLAGEN_EXPLICIT`** (*N* = 20,500 usable in 500–42 ka window): Explicitly reported purified, gelatinised, or ultrafiltered collagen.
- **`COLLAGEN_PROBABLE`** (*N* = 39,178 usable): Undifferentiated bone, tooth, or antler determinations excluding cremated, calcined, or charred specimens (*N* = 3,965 excluded).
- **`CONTROL_ORGANIC`** (*N* = 163,321 usable): Charcoal, wood, seeds, and plant macrofossils.

#### 3.1.3 Role of Organic Controls: Macro-Witnesses vs Micro-Denominators
We state explicitly that the analytical cohort is **size-matched across global space, not site-matched**. Only 388 of 4,695 collagen-bearing sites carry an associated control determination. 

Crucially, material choice in archaeological research is heavily confounded with cultural period: Holocene agricultural settlements are predominantly dated on charcoal and charred seeds, whereas Upper Palaeolithic and Pleistocene megafaunal sites are dated on bone. Consequently, the proportion of dates on bone *rises* monotonically with age (from 23% at <1 ka to >90% at >30 ka). Negative controls therefore **cannot serve as an availability denominator for micro-level survival regressions (such as logistic or Tobit models)**. Instead, their role is as **macro-level physical witnesses**: because charcoal and wood persist and date robustly to 41–42 ka across all warm temperatures (14 °C to 25 °C), the precipitous collapse of bone collagen survival in the exact same warm environments is proven to be a biophysical property of collagen, not an archaeological excavation or preservation artifact.

### 3.2 Paleoclimate Emulation, Cave Buffering & Elevation Lapse Rates
Site-specific thermal histories were reconstructed using the **HadCM3 continuous paleoclimate emulator (Beyer et al. 2020)** [24] across 32,822 unique coordinates. For each coordinate and calibrated calendar age *t*<sub>cal</sub>, the effective integrated degradation temperature T̄ was calculated:

$$\frac{1}{\bar{T} + 273.15} = -\frac{R}{E_a} \ln\left( \frac{1}{t_{\mathrm{cal}}} \int_0^{t_{\mathrm{cal}}} \exp\left[-\frac{E_a}{R(T(t) + 273.15)}\right] dt \right) \tag{9}$$

<s>#### Microclimatic Considerations (Cave Buffering vs Surface Seasonality):
1. **Burial Depth & Cave Attenuation:** Deeply buried fossils and specimens preserved in limestone caves experience the buffered **Mean Annual Ground/Air Temperature (MAT)**, completely insulated from high-frequency diurnal and seasonal thermal extremes.
2. **Surface / Shallow Burial:** In contrast, surface or shallowly buried specimens experience the full amplitude of seasonal fluctuations. Because Arrhenius rates scale exponentially, warm summer months dominate thermal age integration disproportionately.
3. **Elevation Lapse Rates:** Paleoclimate grids (~0.5°–1° resolution) smooth fine-scale mountain topography. In high-altitude karst or montane sites, local temperatures are lower than grid cell averages by the environmental lapse rate (~6.5 °C·km⁻¹). For critical boundary specimens near the radiocarbon wall, both surface-seasonal and cave-buffered temperatures provide rigorous brackets.</s>

#### 3.2.1 Chunk-by-Chunk Paleoclimate Integration & Seasonal Thermal Bounds
Because paleoclimate reconstructions report discrete time-slice blocks (typically 1,000-to-2,000-year intervals in the late Quaternary), thermal history integration is computed chunk-by-chunk. Where a climate epoch exhibits constant mean temperature across a time slice, that slice is integrated as a single thermal unit and accumulated:

$$\text{Age}_{\mathrm{therm}} = \sum_{i=1}^{M} \Delta t_i \cdot \exp\left[ -\frac{E_a}{R} \left( \frac{1}{T_i + 273.15} - \frac{1}{T_{\mathrm{ref}} + 273.15} \right) \right] \tag{10}$$

Crucially, real-world burial environments diverge between buffered underground microclimates and fluctuating surface soils:
1. **Lower Bound (Constant MAT / Cave Buffering):** Limestone caves and deeply buried sedimentary horizons (>1–2 m depth) attenuate high-frequency seasonal oscillations entirely. For verified cave and karst deposits, the burial temperature is rigorously assumed to equal the buffered Mean Annual Ground/Air Temperature (MAT, bioclimatic variable `bio01`). No seasonal fluctuation modeling is required for these samples.
2. **Upper Bound (Surface / Shallow Burial with Seasonal Fluctuations):** Surface bones, open-air scatters, and shallow plowzone burials experience the full amplitude of the annual seasonal cycle (bioclimatic variable `bio07`, annual temperature range). Because chemical degradation rates scale exponentially with temperature via the Arrhenius equation:

$$\langle k \rangle_{\mathrm{annual}} = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_0^{2\pi} A \exp\left[ -\frac{E_a}{R \left( T_{\mathrm{MAT}} + \frac{\Delta T_{\mathrm{range}}}{2}\sin(\theta) + 273.15 \right)} \right] d\theta \tag{11}$$

Because the exponential acceleration during warm summer months vastly outweighs winter retardation, effective surface degradation temperatures (*T*<sub>eff</sub>) are significantly higher than MAT. Table 2 benchmarks this contrast across iconic archaeological sites.

### 5.1 Open Mesoporous Bone vs Straightjacketed Intra-Crystalline Niches
Open cortical bone contains 20–30 vol% porosity with 5–50 nm mesopores that permit groundwater circulation and fragment leaching. Once backbone hydrolysis cleaves collagen into sub-oligomers, mass is rapidly lost to the environment. In contrast, mature enamel, dense dentin, the petrous otic capsule [65], and calcite-cemented karst breccias [44] enclose proteins within tight mineral crystallites (<1% pore volume). Peptide fragments are cleaved but physically retained ("straightjacketed"), enabling mass-spectrometric sequence identification at thermal ages exceeding 10–25 Ma @ 10 °C [19, 20, 45].

### 5.2 The Vexed Question of Dinosaur Collagen & Detection Limits
The recognition of an empirical activation energy of *E*<sub>a</sub> ≈ 80–132 kJ·mol⁻¹ directly illuminates the controversial claims of Mesozoic dinosaur soft-tissue and collagen survival (e.g. Schweitzer and colleagues). 

A critical distinction must be made between:
1. **Bulk Radiocarbon Dating Thresholds:** Requiring >= 0.2–1.0 wt% intact collagen (~10⁻² residual fraction).
2. **Ultrasensitive Mass Spectrometry / ToF-SIMS:** Capable of detecting sub-picomole trace signals (~10⁻⁶ to 10⁻⁸ residual fraction).

However, even assuming an extreme 10⁻⁸ detection threshold and complete physical occlusion within bioapatite crystallites (*E*<sub>a</sub> ≈ 132–142 kJ·mol⁻¹), continuous non-enzymatic peptide bond hydrolysis at non-permafrost terrestrial temperatures (+10 °C to +20 °C) guarantees that all polypeptide backbones degrade to single amino acids or diketopiperazines within **3–5 Ma (open bone)** to **10–25 Ma (closed enamel crystallites)**. Surviving across 66–75 Ma under Mesozoic-to-Cenozoic thermal regimes is thermodynamically impossible without invoking complete mineral replacement or modern laboratory contamination.

## 6. Discussion

### 6.1 Ancient DNA vs Collagen Degradation Kinetics
A striking result of this deconvolution is the remarkable convergence between the empirical activation energy of bone collagen hydrolysis and the degradation kinetics of ancient DNA in bone (Figure 4). 

In their landmark study on 158 radiocarbon-dated moa fossil bones, **Allentoft et al. (2012)** [67] determined the empirical activation energy of mitochondrial DNA degradation in skeletal elements to be ***E*<sub>a</sub> = 127.3 kJ·mol⁻¹** (with an estimated half-life of 521 years for a 242-bp amplicon at 13.1 °C). Foundational solution-phase biochemical measurements by Lindahl (1993) [68] and thermal age integrations by Smith et al. (2003) [12] similarly converged in the range of 120–130 kJ·mol⁻¹ for spontaneous hydrolytic depurination. 

Within experimental uncertainty, the activation energy for whole-bone collagen loss (**Ortner et al. 1972, *E*<sub>a</sub> = 132.1 kJ·mol⁻¹**; open field burial **~82–120 kJ·mol⁻¹**) and ancient DNA degradation (**Allentoft et al. 2012, *E*<sub>a</sub> = 127.3 kJ·mol⁻¹**) are essentially identical (Figure 4). This demonstrates that at ambient burial temperatures on Earth, the degradation of both fundamental biopolymers is governed by a shared energetic barrier: spontaneous, uncatalysed hydrolytic bond cleavage in a condensed aqueous phase, throttled by the physical confinement of the surrounding bioapatite mineral matrix.

#### 6.1.1 Paleogenomic Decay Models: Chemical Damage vs Leaching
Insights from paleogenomic modeling further elucidate how biopolymer loss operates across skeletal tissues. In a meta-analysis of 185 paleogenomic datasets, **Kistler, Ware, Smith, Collins & Allaby (2017)** [72] evaluated the predictive power of environmental and temporal variables on ancient DNA decay. They demonstrated a fundamental duality in degradation dynamics:
1. **Chemical Modification (Deamination):** Base modification (cytosine deamination to uracil) tracks continuous Arrhenius thermal age trajectories with high fidelity.
2. **Fragmentation & Physical Loss:** In contrast, DNA fragment length rapidly reaches a short-length plateau (~30–50 bp) where internal scission rates appear to attenuate, while total amplifiable copy number declines continuously. Kistler et al. [72] concluded that the macroscopic disappearance of DNA from fossil bone is heavily mediated by **bulk diffusion and groundwater leaching** through open mesopores.

This paleogenomic mechanism directly parallels the diagenetic pathway of bone collagen revealed here. In open cortical bone, spontaneous backbone hydrolysis cleaves collagen into short peptide fragments and diketopiperazines (DKPs), which are rapidly flushed from the 20–30 vol% mesoporous void space by percolating groundwater. Consequently, apparent biopolymer loss in open skeletal systems is a compound function of **spontaneous hydrolytic chain scission** (*E*<sub>a</sub> ≈ 82–132 kJ·mol⁻¹) and **hydrodynamic leaching**.

Where physical leaching is prevented—such as inside isolated hydroxyapatite crystal aggregates (Salamon et al. 2005) [45], the hyper-dense otic capsule of the petrous bone (Hansen et al. 2017) [65], the dentino-enamel junction (DEJ) (Cappellini et al. 2019) [20], and calcite-cemented karst breccias (Peters et al. 2023) [44]—the matrix physically straightjackets the biopolymers. In these closed intra-crystalline micro-environments, cleaved fragments cannot diffuse away, allowing authentic protein and DNA sequences to persist into deep time far beyond open-system predictions.

### 6.2 Predictive Scope: Upper-Bound Frontier vs Environmental Realities
The Arrhenius boundary established here represents the **theoretical upper-bound preservation envelope for the most pristine, uncompromised skeletal remains**. In real-world archaeological contexts, the vast majority of fossil bones will fail to reach this thermal ceiling due to extrinsic destructive pathways:
- **Microbial Bioerosion:** Early post-mortem invasion by collagenolytic bacteria and saprophytic fungi that bore microscopic focal destructions (MFDs) and digest collagen enzymatically [14, 28].
- **Mineral Dissolution & Acid Leaching:** Acidic groundwater that dissolves the protective bioapatite shell, exposing collagen fibrils to catastrophic swelling and accelerated hydrolysis [31].
- **Thermal Alteration (Cooking / Burning):** Anthropogenic heating above *T*<sub>m</sub> that triggers instantaneous cooperative gelatinisation [60, 61].
- **Mechanical Fragmentation:** Soil compaction and weathering that shatter macro-porosity.

Consequently, limestone karst environments (e.g. South African caves, European karst, tropical Queensland breccias) represent the most attractive geochemical targets for deep-time sequence recovery, as neutral-to-alkaline pH and carbonate saturation prevent mineral dissolution while cave microclimates provide stable, buffered temperatures.

## 7. Conclusion: From Deep Time to Human Physiological Aging

By compiling and deconvolving over 313,000 global radiocarbon determinations with 120,000-year continuous paleoclimate reconstructions, we have resolved the two-decade discrepancy between high-temperature laboratory heating and empirical deep-time fossil preservation. Instrumental radiocarbon blank truncation at ~42 ka BP imposes an artificial ceiling in cold environments (T̄ < 13.8 °C), while the uncensored warm regime demonstrates that open bone collagen degradation proceeds under an effective activation barrier of *E*<sub>a</sub> ≈ 82–132 kJ·mol⁻¹, consistent with uncatalysed peptide bond hydrolysis and intact-bone laboratory models.

### 7.1 Spontaneous Non-Enzymatic Collagen Hydrolysis in Living Human Tissues
A profound biological conclusion emerges when these empirical kinetics are translated from archaeological deep time into living human physiology at **37 °C (310.15 K)**.

Collagen is the most abundant protein in the human body, comprising approximately 30% of total protein mass (~3 to 4 kg of collagen in an adult human). Each collagen monomer is composed of three polypeptide chains of ~1,055 amino acids each, containing approximately 3,162 peptide bonds per triple helix. In total, the human collagenous extracellular matrix contains on the order of **2.5 × 10²⁵ peptide bonds**.

While metabolically active tissues undergo rapid cellular turnover, seminal radiocarbon bomb-pulse and amino acid racemisation studies by **Heinemeier et al. (2016)** [69] and **Ritz-Timme and colleagues (2002, 2003)** [70, 71] have demonstrated that collagen in **articular cartilage, dentin, tendons, and intervertebral discs exhibits essentially zero turnover throughout adult human life**. 

Extrapolating our empirical hydrolytic rate constants (*k* ≈ 10⁻¹⁰ to 10⁻¹¹ s⁻¹ at 37 °C) across this vast molecular pool reveals that **between 20 and 200 million peptide bonds undergo spontaneous, non-enzymatic hydrolytic scission across the human body every single day**. In tissues devoid of metabolic renewal:
1. Spontaneous backbone hydrolyses accumulate irreversibly over decades.
2. Free N-termini adjacent to proline/hydroxyproline undergo spontaneous intramolecular cyclisation to form diketopiperazines (DKPs), excising dipeptide fragments.
3. This continuous, unrepairable chemical attrition disrupts load-bearing fibrillar networks, providing a fundamental biophysical clock and mechanical driver for **age-related tissue fatigue, tendon rupture, and osteoarthritis**.

The same chemical laws of spontaneous peptide bond hydrolysis that dictate the ultimate survival horizon of Pleistocene hominins and Pliocene Arctic megafauna thus govern the inevitable mechanical aging of the human body.
